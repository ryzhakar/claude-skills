## I. LEONARD'S OWN ACCOUNT OF HIS SYSTEM

### On the condition itself

"I have no short-term memory. I know who I am and all about myself, but since my injury I can't make any new memories. Everything fades. If we talk for too long, I'll forget how we started. I don't know if we've ever met before, and the next time I see you I won't remember this conversation. So if I seem strange or rude, that's probably..."
— To Burt, scene 13

"Like waking. Like you always just woke up."
— To Burt, on what it's like, scene 13

"I don't have amnesia. I remember everything about myself up until the incident."
— To Teddy, scene 115

"I can't make new memories. Everything fades, nothing sticks. By the time we finish this conversation I won't remember how it started, and the next time I see you I won't know that I've ever met you before."
— To Natalie, scene 139

"No, Officer, but with my condition, you don't know anything... you feel angry, guilty, you don't know why. You could do something terrible and not have the faintest idea ten minutes later."
— Phone call, scene C140

### On the core design principle: facts over memory

"Memory's unreliable. No, really. Memory's not perfect. It's not even that good. Ask the police, eyewitness testimony is unreliable. The cops don't catch a killer by sitting around remembering stuff. They collect facts, make notes, draw conclusions. Facts, not memories: that's how you investigate. I know, it's what I used to do. Memory can change the shape of a room or the color of a car. It's an interpretation, not a record. Memories can be changed or distorted and they're irrelevant if you have the facts."
— To Teddy, diner scene 44

### On his differentiation from Sammy

"Sammy Jankis wrote himself endless notes. But he'd get mixed up. I've got a more graceful solution to the memory problem. I'm disciplined and organized. I use habit and routine to make my life possible. Sammy had no drive. No reason to make it work. Me? I gotta reason."
— Phone call, scene 22

"Conditioning didn't work for Sammy, so he became helpless. But it works for me. I live the way Sammy couldn't. Habit and Conditioning. Acting on instinct."
— Phone call, scene 55

"So Sammy can't learn any new skills. But I find something in my research: Conditioning. Sammy should still be able to learn through repetition. It's how you learn stuff like riding a bike, things you don't think about, you just get better through practice. Call it muscle memory, whatever, but it's a completely different part of the brain from the short-term memory."
— Phone call, scene 47

### On motivation as system anchor

"Me? I gotta reason."
— Phone call, scene 22

"He took away the woman I love and he took away my memory. He destroyed everything; my life and my ability to live."
— To Teddy, diner scene 44

"Just for revenge. That's what keeps me going. It's all I have."
— To Teddy, diner scene 44

### On meaning and action without memory

"The world doesn't disappear when you close your eyes, does it? My actions still have meaning, even if I can't remember them. My wife deserves vengeance, and it doesn't make any difference whether I know about it."
— To Natalie, scene 24

"I have to believe in the world outside my own mind. I have to believe that my actions still have meaning, even if I can't remember them. I have to believe that when my eyes are closed, the world's still there."
— V.O., scene 182

"We all need mirrors to remind ourselves who we are. I'm no different."
— V.O., scene 182

### On what can still be known

"There are things you know for sure. I know the feel of the world. I know how this wood will sound when I knock. I know how this glass will feel when I pick it up. Certainties. You think it's knowledge, but it's a kind of memory, a kind you take for granted. I can remember so much. I know the feel of the world, and I know her."
— To Natalie, couch scene 57

### On what kind of memory remains for his wife

"You can only feel details. Bits and pieces which you didn't bother to put into words. And extreme moments you feel even if you don't want to. Put it together and you get the feel of the person, enough to know how much you miss them, and how much you hate the person who took them away."
— V.O. during wife flashback, scene 25

### On interpersonal reading as a live compensatory skill

"I had to see through people's bullshit. It was useful experience, because now it's my life. When I meet someone, I don't even know if I've met them before. I have to look in their eyes and just figure them out. My job taught me that the best way to find out what someone knew was to let them talk."
— Phone call, scene 28

"Throw in the occasional 'why?' but just listen. And watch the eyes, the body language."
— V.O. over insurance montage, scene 29

"It's complicated. You might catch a sign but attach the wrong meaning to it. If someone touches their nose while they're talking, experts will tell you it means they're lying. It really means they're nervous, and people get nervous for all sorts of reasons. It's all about context."
— V.O. over insurance montage, scene 29

"I can read people and I'm thinking bad actor."
— On Sammy, scene 41

### On face-to-face as communication protocol

"I'm not good on the phone."
— To Burt, scene 13

"You said you like to look people in the eye when you talk to them."
— Burt quoting Leonard's own prior instruction back to him, scene 13

"I'm not too good on the phone. I need to look people in the eye when I talk to them."
— To Burt (again), scene 131

### On the face-reading requirement for unobstructed view

"Could you take off your sunglasses? It's just hard for me—"
— To Natalie at City Grill, scene 24. Face-reading requires direct eye contact; obstructions degrade the read.

### On social performance as management strategy

"Now I know. You take it. If you think you're supposed to recognize someone, you pretend to. You bluff it to get a pat on the head from the doctors. You bluff it to seem less of a freak."
— Phone call, scene 144. Social recognition is performed even when absent; the performance manages others' responses.

### On managing eating without memory

"It's my condition. I never know if I've already eaten, so I always just eat small amounts."
— To Teddy, diner scene 44

"It's weird, but if you don't eat for a while then your body stops being hungry. You get sort of shaky but you don't realize you haven't eaten."
— To Teddy, diner scene 44

### On the present and what can be noted

"She's gone and the present is trivia, which I can scribble down as notes."
— To Natalie, scene 57

### On the limits of the system with emotional memory

"Probably tried this before. Probably burned truckloads of your stuff. Can't remember to forget you."
— V.O. at reservoir, scene 95. The system cannot process grief; burning her possessions is repeated because the forgetting resets the need.

### On the waking orientation monologue (scripted, second person)

"So where are you? You're in some motel room... you don't know how long you've been there, or how you got there... Just some anonymous motel room. Won't tell you anything. Nothing in the drawers, but you look anyway. Nothing except the Gideon Bible."
— V.O., scenes 2 and 5

### On the moment of choosing to perpetuate the system

"I'm not a killer... but right now I need to be."
— V.O., scene 180

"Maybe I'm not finished yet. Maybe I need to be sure that you won't ever use me again."
— V.O., scene 180

"You're a John G.? Fine, then you can be my John G."
— V.O., scene 180

"Do I lie to myself to be happy? In your case, Teddy... yes, I will."
— V.O., scene 180

### On the system cycling back to its start

"Now... where was I?"
— Final V.O., scene 183. Spoken upon seeing a tattoo parlor sign. The system is re-entered.

## II. SHORT STORY: EARL/NARRATOR'S ACCOUNT OF THE SYSTEM

### On lists as the foundational tool

"Lists are the only way out of this mess."

"The only way out of this mess, of course, is to take steps to ensure that you control the idiots that you become. To take your chain gang, hand in hand, and lead them. The best way to do this is with a list. It's like a letter you write to yourself. A master plan, drafted by the guy who can see the light, made with steps simple enough for the rest of the idiots to understand. Follow steps one through one hundred. Repeat as necessary."

### On placement as the critical design constraint

"And the secret, of course, to any list is to keep it in a place where you're bound to see it."

### On the Chinese room model of self-instruction

"It's like that computer thing, the Chinese room... One guy sits in a little room, laying down cards with letters written on them in a language he doesn't understand, laying them down one letter at a time in a sequence according to someone else's instructions. The cards are supposed to spell out a joke in Chinese. The guy doesn't speak Chinese, of course. He just follows his instructions."

"There are some obvious differences in your situation, of course: You broke out of the room they had you in, so the whole enterprise has to be portable. And the guy giving the instructions — that's you, too, just an earlier version of you."

### On the condition as a version of the universal human problem

"People, even regular people, are never just any one person with one set of attributes. We're all at the mercy of the limbic system, clouds of electricity drifting through the brain. Every man is broken into twenty-four-hour fractions, and then again within those twenty-four hours. It's a daily pantomime, one man yielding control to the next... Every man is a mob, a chain gang of idiots."

"Because for a few minutes of every day, every man becomes a genius. Moments of clarity, insight, whatever you want to call them... But then the genius, the savant, has to hand over the controls to the next guy down the pike... and insight and brilliance and salvation are all entrusted to a moron or a hedonist or a narcoleptic."

### On the advantage of the condition

"Your advantage in forgetting is that you'll forget to write yourself off as a lost cause."

### On time and the condition

"Time is three things for most people, but for you, for us, just one. A singularity. One moment. This moment. Like you're the center of the clock, the axis on which the hands turn. Time moves about you but never moves you."

"Close your eyes and you can start all over again. Conjure up that necessary emotion, fresh as roses."

### On the future self as a separate person

"It's a shame, really, that you and I will never meet. But, like the song says, 'By the time you read this note, I'll be gone.'"

"I don't know where you'll be when you read this. I'm not even sure if you'll bother to read this."

### On completion as a condition of rest

"But only if you've got a little piece of paper in your hand that says you got him."

## III. PHYSICAL ARTIFACTS

### A. The Polaroid Camera

- Carried on a strap over the shoulder; on his person during operations
- Primary input device for the system
- Capture is always followed by annotation: write on the photo while it develops
- The broad white strip on the bottom and back of each Polaroid is an existing feature of the format; Leonard exploits it
- The fanning motion: Leonard fans the photo during development to accelerate it (scene 1, reversed)

### B. Polaroid Photographs — Structure

Every Polaroid has:
- **Front, bottom strip**: name / identifier / label / contact info — written after capture, while developing
- **Back strip**: notes, instructions, warnings, behavioral directives, motivational analysis

The Polaroid image itself is fixed upon capture and cannot be revised. Only the text strips are revisable.

Uses of a Polaroid during a live session:
1. **Real-time name recall**: In scene 4, Teddy says "You read it off your fucking photo. You don't know me." Leonard has looked at the front strip to produce Teddy's name during the confrontation. The Polaroid substitutes for a memory he does not have.
2. **Pre-encounter briefing**: In scenes 23 and 36, before entering the City Grill, Leonard pulls out Natalie's Polaroid, flips to the back, and reads "SHE HAS ALSO LOST SOMEONE, SHE WILL HELP YOU OUT OF PITY" — instruction for the imminent encounter, read at the threshold before entering.
3. **Navigation**: The Discount Inn Polaroid with address written beneath is consulted while driving (scene 19).
4. **Car identification**: The Jaguar Polaroid is held up to prove ownership against Teddy's misdirection (scene 7). In scene 44: Leonard reaches into his pocket and takes out the Discount Inn Polaroid when Teddy asks where he's staying — he doesn't remember the motel name but the Polaroid tells him.
5. **Contact enabling**: The phone number on Teddy's front strip (TEL. 555 0134) allows Leonard to initiate an outbound call in scene 21, producing a face-to-face meeting.
6. **Social signaling**: Leonard leaves the Teddy Polaroid on Burt's counter for Burt to tap when Teddy arrives (scene 6).
7. **Identity verification**: Polaroid image cross-referenced against documentary license photo (scene 21).

### D. Tattoos — System Overview

Tattoos are the most permanent tier. Reserved for facts that will not change and must persist across all sessions regardless of what happens to notes, pockets, or wallet. Cannot be removed by others. Cannot be lost. Cannot fall out.

The tattoos exist in multiple styles: "some CRUDE, some ELABORATE. The messages run in all directions, some UPSIDEDOWN, some BACKWARDS." (scene 21) — different times, different hands, different orientations serving different reading conditions.

- Readable at any moment without removing clothing

**Other forearm:**

- "FACT 4. LASTNAME: G-------"

**Belly (upside-down — readable when Leonard looks down):**
- "PHOTOGPAPH: HOUSE, CAR, FRIEND, FOE"

**Chest (backwards — readable only in mirror):**

- Requires a mirror or another person to turn him around to read
- The mirror is therefore a functional reading tool in the system
- Not readable by a casual observer looking at him; a form of privacy

**Blank area above heart:**
- No tattoo

## IV. BEHAVIORAL PROTOCOLS

### A. The Waking Orientation Sequence

Scripted, rehearsed, delivered in second person. Enacted every time Leonard regains consciousness:

1. Open eyes; roll them to one side; turn head
2. State location type: "You're in some motel room."
3. Survey the room as if for the first time
4. Check dresser drawers — expect to find nothing
5. Check bedside table drawer — expect only the Gideon Bible
   - If Bible present: normal motel room confirmed
   - If Bible present with gun on top (scene 67, Dodd's room): anomaly detected; prior self has done something here. V.O. script pauses: "Never anything but the Gideon... [pulls drawer, pauses] ...Bible." The pause is where the deviation is registered.
6. Check room key on dresser — read room number from plastic tag
7. Check back of hand for "REMEMBER SAMMY JANKIS"
8. Remove shirt; check tattoos systematically
9. Pull Polaroids from pocket; flip through to establish who and where

The V.O. uses second person throughout ("So where are you?") not first person. The waking self is addressed as a separate entity requiring instruction. This is a designed address mode, not a stylistic accident.

### B. The V.O. Mode Switch

Leonard's V.O. operates in two modes:
- **First person** ("I gotta reason," "I found you, you fuck"): normal active processing
- **Second person** ("So where are you?", "You're in some motel room"): orientation mode — Leonard addressing himself as a stranger who must be briefed

The switch to second person occurs at moments of acute disorientation, principally on waking. It marks transition from unknown state to re-established orientation.

### C. Real-Time V.O. as Mid-Action Orientation

During the chase with Dodd (scenes 84/87), Leonard narrates his own situation to himself in real time as new information arrives:
- "What the fuck am I doing?"
- "Chasing him!"
- "FUCK! He's chasing me."

This is the second-person orientation mechanism operating during action rather than on waking. When continuity breaks mid-session, the V.O. re-establishes it.

### D. The Pocket Check Protocol

When confused or entering a new situation, Leonard empties his pockets methodically to reconstruct the situation from what he finds:
- Scene 69 (Dodd's room): empties pockets onto dresser, finds Dodd Polaroid with instructions
- Scene 151 (tattoo parlor cubicle): feels in pocket, finds charred Polaroid and beer mat
- Scene 3: "patting his jacket pockets" before entering the derelict building — quick tactile confirmation of resources before action

### E. The Pre-Encounter Polaroid Briefing

Before entering a social encounter with a known person, Leonard pulls the relevant Polaroid and reads the back:
- Scene 23: stops outside City Grill door, checks note against restaurant name, pulls out Polaroids, finds Natalie's, flips to back, reads "SHE HAS ALSO LOST SOMEONE, SHE WILL HELP YOU OUT OF PITY" — then enters
- Scene 36: identical sequence; checks restaurant name, flips through to Natalie's Polaroid

This is a threshold step: check back-of-photo instructions before entering the encounter, not during it.

### F. The Polaroid Capture Protocol

1. Encounter person or place requiring memory
2. Take photograph immediately — before any other action. (Scene 167/168: Leonard kills Jimmy, then looks for his camera before anything else.)
3. Fan photo during development if urgent
4. Write identifier on front white strip while developing
5. Write notes/instructions on back strip
6. Place in jacket pocket or on wall chart

Social framing when photographing a person: "Something to remember you by." (scenes 130 and 156) — normalizes the act.

### G. Tattoo Verification Protocol

When establishing whether a target matches tattooed facts:
1. Obtain documentary evidence (registration, license)
2. Remove shirt, expose forearms
3. Verbally check each fact against document: "White... male. First name... John. Last name... G for Gammell. Drugs. License plate. SG... 13... 7... IU. It's him. It's actually him." (scene 21)
4. Cross-reference Polaroid image against license photo simultaneously
5. Only after verification: write on Polaroid back and prepare to act

Two independent sources cross-referenced. Neither alone is treated as sufficient.

### H. The Shaving/Tattooing Preparation Cascade

A multi-step sequence set up by a prior self for a future self. Each element triggers the next:

1. Note "SHAVE" taped to right thigh (scene 10/14) — found on waking
2. Remove note, proceed to bathroom
3. In bathroom: white paper bag with "SHAVE THIGH" — confirms and specifies the instruction
4. Bag contains: shaving foam + disposable razors — supplies already gathered
5. Ice bucket already present and steaming (prepared by prior self) for razor cleaning between passes
6. Shave right thigh
7. Result: skin prepared for tattooing

The prior self cannot tattoo in this session; they set up conditions for the future self to proceed. The chain is: note → bathroom → bag → supplies → ice bucket → shave → skin ready.

### I. The File Card Staging Protocol

Between provisional knowledge and permanent inscription:
1. Write tentative tattoo content on file card
2. Continue gathering evidence
3. Revise file card if new information changes the formulation
4. Only after verification: take card to tattoo parlor
5. Alternatively: rip up card if decision is reversed (scene 180 — "TATTOO: I'VE DONE IT" written then destroyed)

### J. Call-Blocking Protocol

- Instructs Burt to hold all calls
- Exception: Teddy gets through
- Stated reason: phone unreliable; can't see the person
- Permanent override rule: "NEVER ANSWER THE PHONE" tattooed on left arm
- Override exception: when "TAKE MY CALL" envelope arrives under door
- Scene 124: sees tattoo → lets phone ring → phone stops → checks room number → calls Burt to reinstate no-calls instruction

### K. Room Number Verification Before Outgoing Calls

Scene 124: Leonard reads "NEVER ANSWER THE PHONE" tattoo, lets the phone ring out, then goes to the door, opens it, reads the room number ("21"), returns, and calls Burt identifying himself as "Mr. Shelby in Room 21." He verifies his location before communicating it to others.

### L. Communication Setup Protocol

When needing a future meeting:
1. State preference for face-to-face
2. Ask other party to write the meeting details on a note
3. Put note in pocket
4. Future self finds note and acts on it

Writing is delegated to the other party when they are the source of the information and a pen is available. Scene 50: Natalie takes her eye pencil and writes the meeting note on the pharmacy bag — she provides both writing instrument and writing. Scene 120: Leonard has paper but no pen; asks Natalie for a pen, then hands her both paper and pen and asks her to write Dodd's details. This is a workaround for pen absence, not a designed protocol. The designed principle is that Leonard writes his own records.

### M. Pre-Mission Preparation Protocol

Scene 11: before leaving room 304 with the gun:
1. Stick Teddy Polaroid between teeth (frees hands)
2. Hold handgun up, check it is loaded
3. Stick gun in back of waistband
4. Put photo in jacket pocket
5. Sling Polaroid camera over shoulder

A physical pre-departure checklist enacted before the session of action begins.

### N. Pre-Social Preparation Ritual

Scene 12: Leonard leaves room 304, walks to office, then "pauses just outside the glass door, breathing, psyching himself up." A mental preparation moment before entering a social/operational situation. Not documented anywhere in the system — purely behavioral.

### O. Room Setup Protocol

Scene 112, described as "well-practiced, efficient movements":
1. Enter room
2. Take Polaroid of motel exterior, write address on it
3. Unpack sports bag
4. Unroll wall chart, mount on wall
5. Work through Polaroid stack placing each photo in its established position "like someone playing solitaire"
6. Set up working documents

"Well-practiced, efficient movements" — the room setup has been performed enough times to become procedural memory. This is the conditioning principle Leonard describes, applied to his own setup routine.

### P. Eating Protocol

- Small amounts only, regardless of hunger or appetite signals
- Reason: cannot know if already eaten
- Body's hunger signal unreliable after extended non-eating

### Q. Physical Environment Reading (Sensory)

Scene 100: Leonard wakes and "slides his hand onto the other half of the bed, feeling the residual warmth, smiling." He reads recent presence of another person through physical sensation — warmth as evidence of recency. Not a designed protocol; a retained human behavior applied to the condition.

### R. Physical Inspection of Evidence

Scene 3: Leonard inspects the pickup "with a methodical, practiced eye." Reads physical traces: condition of truck, tire tracks, bullets on passenger seat — drawn conclusions: "These tracks aren't more than a few days old." Insurance investigator methodology applied to the environment.

### S. The Escort Simulation Protocol

Scene 112/113:
1. Use Yellow Pages to hire a blonde escort
2. Give her the shopping bag of wife's possessions
3. Instruct her to arrange possessions around the room as if they were hers: "Just pretend these things are yours, and this is your bedroom."
4. Go to bed together
5. She waits until he falls asleep
6. Goes to bathroom, shuts door "just loud enough to wake me up"
7. Leonard wakes to the sound; the possessions, the warmth, the sound of someone in the next room produce the felt experience of his wife's presence

This is not information storage, navigation, or fact-gathering. It is emotional/experiential simulation using a human actor and props. A distinct category of system element.

### T. People-Reading Protocol (live, session-by-session)

In every new encounter:
- Look them in the eyes
- Let them talk; "the best way to find out what someone knew was to let them talk"
- Throw in the occasional "why?"
- Watch hands, neck, micro-behaviors — close-ups of fiddling hands and neck-scratching in the insurance montage (scene 29)
- Read for context, not isolated signals: "It's all about context"
- Operates in parallel with the written record system, not as a substitute for it

### U. Social Performance Protocol

When meeting someone new who appears to expect recognition: pretend to recognize them. "If you think you're supposed to recognize someone, you pretend to. You bluff it to get a pat on the head from the doctors. You bluff it to seem less of a freak." (scene 144)

Limits friction in social encounters; allows the interaction to proceed without explanation in cases where explanation would be more disruptive.

### V. The Condition Explanation Script

When a new contact needs to understand the condition, Leonard deploys a consistent explanation:
- "I have no short-term memory. I know who I am and all about myself, but since my injury I can't make any new memories. Everything fades..."
- Deployed to Burt (scene 13), Natalie (scene 24), Dodd's motel context (scene 22 phone), Ferdy's bar (scene 139), and others
- The acknowledgment by others that they've heard it before serves as confirmation of prior relationship: "I guess I've told you about my condition." / "Only every time I see ya!" (Teddy, scene 6)

### W. Name Correction as Session-Persistent Behavior

"Please call me Leonard. My wife called me Lenny."
— Consistent across scenes 24 and 120. Session-to-session, Leonard corrects the nickname to his full name. This is a stable self-identification behavior that persists without notes.

### X. Deletion Protocol for Polaroids

- Soft deletion: scribble over back text — original message gone but artifact remains; new text can be written over or alongside
- Hard deletion: burn — "You have to burn them." (scene 57). They cannot be torn; plastic too strong.
- Partial destruction: the Jimmy Polaroid is burned but an arm remains. Leonard keeps the remnant in his jacket pocket.

### Y. Car Alarm as Locator

Scene 84: Leonard is running and cannot see the Jaguar. He "pulls his KEYS OUT and hits the ALARM switch. Hearing the DOUBLE BEEP as the alarm stops, he spots the Jaguar." He uses the key remote to trigger the alarm auditorily to locate the car in a parking lot he cannot scan visually.

### B. Room 21 (old room — not a deliberately designed second space)

### D. The Gideon Bible as Baseline

Its presence alongside a gun (scene 67, Dodd's room): the V.O. script runs until disrupted by the unexpected — "Never anything but the Gideon... [pause] ...Bible." — the pause marks the deviation. The Bible is a known-constant against which anomaly is detectable.

## VI. THE PERMANENCE HIERARCHY

As enacted in the text:

1. **Body tattoos** — most permanent. For confirmed, unchanging target facts and core rules. Cannot be altered without professional intervention. Cannot be removed by others.
2. **Indelible body writing** — "REMEMBER SAMMY JANKIS." Resists washing. Not tattooed. More accessible than tattoos; present at all times on the back of the hand.
3. **Polaroid photographs** — semi-permanent. Image fixed at capture; text strips revisable (scribble) or destroyable (burning).
4. **Wall chart** — semi-permanent. Structural index, rebuilt each room change from established components with established positions.
5. **Police file with conclusions** — semi-permanent document. Compressed over time; summary maintained separately on the back.
6. **Notes in pockets** — temporary. Found and acted on; session-specific.
7. **Notes taped to body** — temporary-to-session. Removed when executed.
8. **File cards** — temporary planning artifacts. Revised mid-session; discarded after tattoo, or destroyed if decision reverses.

## VIII. IMPLICIT AUTHENTICATION MECHANISMS

Not designed as security features; function as verification in practice:

**Handwriting recognition as authorship check:**
Scene 34: confronted with a room Burt says isn't his, Leonard picks up the paper bag and says "Then why is this my handwriting?" — he uses his own handwriting as proof of prior presence and authorship. This is his stated authorship verification mechanism.

**Prior own-writing outranks current external recommendation:**
Scene 115: Leonard writes "DON'T TRUST HER" on Natalie's Polaroid at Teddy's instigation. He then reads his own prior note "DON'T BELIEVE HIS LIES" on Teddy's Polaroid. He trusts his own prior recorded assessment of Teddy over Teddy's current verbal input, and scribbles out "DON'T TRUST HER." The hierarchy: own prior written note > current external recommendation.

**"Sammy" as implicit recognition token:**
Scene 115: Leonard grabs Teddy by the throat. Teddy says "Sammy. Remember Sammy. You told me about Sammy." Leonard releases him. Knowing Sammy signals prior relationship. Not designed as authentication but functions as one.

**The condition explanation acknowledgment as prior-relationship confirmation:**
When a new contact acknowledges having heard the condition explanation before, that acknowledgment is evidence of prior contact. Teddy: "Only every time I see ya!" — confirmation of a relationship the current Leonard cannot remember.

**Cross-source verification:**
When establishing a target match, Leonard uses two independent sources simultaneously: Polaroid image against license photo, and tattooed facts against registration document. Neither alone is treated as sufficient.

**The authorship vulnerability:**
The handwriting check works for distinguishing Leonard's own writing from unrecognized writing. It fails in three cases: (a) in a future session, Leonard cannot determine whether a note of unfamiliar handwriting was legitimately received from an identified source in a prior session or planted by an adversary; (b) when Leonard delegates writing to another person due to pen absence, that person's handwriting enters the system in a known-source capacity that he will not remember; (c) foreign writing in the envelope (Natalie's derelict building address) and as notes is received in the moment with known source — that source knowledge does not persist.

## IX. WRITING INSTRUMENT DEPENDENCY

The paper note and Polaroid annotation systems are entirely contingent on having a pen:

## XI. THE SAMMY JANKIS NARRATIVE AS SYSTEM COMPONENT

Not background story. Multi-function operational element:

1. **Explanatory script**: told to every new person to explain the condition and pre-empt confusion. Consistent in substance across every scene where it appears.
2. **Self-orientation narrative**: "Sammy's story helps me understand my own situation." (scene 22)
3. **Design contrast**: Sammy is defined as the wrong approach. Notes without discipline. No conditioning. No drive. No reason. Leonard defines his own system in opposition to Sammy's failure.
4. **Conditioning anchor**: "REMEMBER SAMMY JANKIS" written on his hand — the lesson is present at all times.
5. **Social lubricant**: provides a narrative form new contacts can grasp quickly.
6. **Possible displacement**: stage directions for Sammy scenes read "(LEONARD TO SUBSTITUTE)" and "(LEONARD'S WIFE TO SUBSTITUTE)." The script explicitly marks the ambiguity about whether the Sammy story is Leonard's own history externalized. Not resolved in the screenplay.
7. **Vulnerability surface**: Jimmy's dying words — "Sammy... remember Sammy..." (scene 169) — the one moment in the script where an external voice invokes the Sammy name in the context of Leonard's own guilt. Leonard is SHOCKED. He does not resolve what this means before Jimmy is silent.
8. **The Sammy story as possible conditioning**: Teddy, scene 174: "Like you've told yourself. Over and over. Conditioning yourself to believe. 'Learning through repetition.'" — the narrative Leonard tells others may be the mechanism by which he has conditioned himself to believe it.

## XII. STRUCTURAL ABSENCES

**No timestamps on any artifact:**
- No dates on notes, Polaroids, or files
- No sequence numbers
- Leonard cannot determine how old a note is, how long ago a Polaroid was taken, or how far into the investigation he is
- Scene 4 V.O.: "I've finally found him. How long have I been looking?" — he does not know

**Authorship verification: principle exists but has a specific vulnerability:**
- Leonard's stated principle is "facts, not recommendations" — he trusts his own documented facts over others' verbal input
- His own handwriting is the authorship marker (scene 34: "Then why is this my handwriting?")
- Scene 115: his own prior note outranks Teddy's current recommendation
- The vulnerability: in a fresh session, Leonard cannot determine whether a note of unfamiliar handwriting was (a) legitimately received from an identified source in a prior session or (b) planted by an adversary. Legitimately received foreign writing loses its identified-source status across sessions.

**No cross-session consistency checking:**
- No mechanism to detect contradiction between a note from one session and a note from another
- Each session operates from whatever artifacts it finds

**Wording inconsistency across sessions:**
- "DON'T LISTEN TO HIS LIES" (scene 4) vs "DON'T BELIEVE HIS LIES" (scene 11) — both appear in the source on the same Polaroid in different scenes; no explanation is given
- License plate: "SG13 1NU" (scene 50) vs "SG13 7IU" (all other scenes) — internal discrepancy in the source text as written

**No cover when the pen is absent:**
- The paper and Polaroid annotation systems fail completely without a writing instrument
- Tattoos and existing artifacts are immune; new information cannot be recorded

## XIII. STATED DESIGN PRINCIPLES

Distilled from Sections I and II, stated in Leonard's or the narrator's own words:

1. Facts, not memories
2. Collect evidence → make notes → draw conclusions
3. Habit and routine as substitute for continuous memory
4. Conditioning through repetition — learn by doing, not by remembering
5. Place artifacts where they will be intercepted before the wrong action
6. External record-keeping over internal memory
7. The future self is a separate person; address them as such
8. Motivation (purpose) is what makes the system work; without it the system fails — Sammy had no reason
9. The body is the most secure storage medium
10. Face-to-face over phone; reading the person requires seeing them
11. Read people live; documentation supplements but does not replace direct observation
12. A tattoo requires a deliberate intermediate step (file card) before commitment
13. Photograph first, annotate after
14. The system must be portable
